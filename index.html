Hugo Tierrablanca

Research Associate @ Harvard University

Hi, I'm Hugo! I'm a Research Associate at Harvard University, working with Prof. Himabindu Lakkaraju on AI safety and reliable high-stakes decision systems.

My current work studies the selective labels problem and how to build faithful evaluation pipelines when labels are missing or biased. I am especially interested in combining interpretability, theory, and practical deployment constraints.

Previously, I graduated from Harvard University in May 2025 with a B.A. in Applied Mathematics. I am currently on a gap period and plan to apply to Ph.D. programs.

Research Interests

My research spans AI safety and capabilities, with a focus on building AI systems that are both more powerful and more trustworthy. On the safety side, I am interested in mechanistic interpretability - reverse-engineering the internal structure of neural networks - and in alignment, ensuring systems reliably act in accordance with human values even as they grow more capable. On the capabilities side, I am interested in the full stack of intelligent behavior: learning structured representations, reasoning causally, modeling world dynamics, and acting in the physical world.

Interests listed in no particular order

How do we reverse-engineer the internal cognitive structures of neural networks - treating them as complex systems with distinct "biology," developmental stages, and controllable internal states?

I'm interested in interpretability not just as a safety tool, but as a scientific program: understanding what models have actually learned, at the level of weights and activations. My current work on data attribution and influence functions connects here - tracing model behavior back to the training examples that shaped it.

Discovery

Model Biology & Circuit Analysis - Mapping the "connectome" of large models to identify universal cognitive motifs - induction heads, inhibition circuits, memory retrieval mechanisms - that implement specific capabilities. This includes using tractable model organisms (Othello-GPT, algorithmic toy tasks) to establish ground-truth mechanisms before scaling to frontier systems.

Developmental Interpretability - Studying the "embryology" of models by tracking how circuits and capabilities emerge over training time, specifically analyzing phase transitions and feature formation dynamics (e.g., Singular Learning Theory).

Geometry

The Geometry of Representations - Investigating the high-dimensional geometry of feature encoding: the Linear Representation Hypothesis, Superposition (packing N features into M < N dimensions), and the role of Privileged Bases in disentangling representations.

Intervention

Activation Steering & Internals-Influenced Training - Developing techniques to control model behavior from the inside out: activation steering to clamp specific feature vectors at inference time, and internals-influenced training to regularize internal states during learning - explicitly penalizing deceptive pathways or encouraging disentangled representations and doing so safely.

Agentic & Temporal Circuit Analysis - Extending interpretability beyond single-token prediction to understand how models maintain long-horizon state, represent hierarchical goals, and fail when internal plans drift or collapse.

Frontiers

Generative & Dynamics Interpretability - Extending mechanistic analysis to any architecture where computation unfolds over time or iterative refinement: World Models encoding environmental rules, diffusion and flow-matching processes constructing complex data, and architectures yet to be developed - wherever the question shifts from "what did the model predict?" to "how did it simulate?"

Scalable & Next-Generation Methods - Extending tools like Sparse Autoencoders (SAEs) and researching novel decomposition frameworks with superior fidelity and scalability for interpreting massive models.

Why it's hard

Circuits emerge from gradient descent with no guarantee of modularity or human-legible structure, findings from small models often fail to transfer to larger ones, and there is no ground truth against which to validate a proposed circuit explanation.

How do we ensure powerful AI systems reliably act in accordance with human intent and values - even as they become more capable than their supervisors?

I'm interested in the theoretical and empirical foundations of alignment: how we specify what we want, verify that a model has understood it, and detect when it hasn't. My work on selective labels and evaluation bias connects here - if we cannot faithfully evaluate a model's behavior, we cannot certify that it is aligned. Specific interests:

Scalable Oversight - Developing methods for humans to supervise systems smarter than them, including AI-assisted evaluation, Recursive Reward Modeling, and Debate.

Value Learning & IRL - Algorithms that infer human preferences by observing behavior, rather than requiring explicit reward specification.

Eliciting Latent Knowledge (ELK) - Training models to truthfully report their internal state, even when deception might yield a higher reward.

Why it's hard

We cannot enumerate all deployment contexts in advance, so specifying and verifying intended behavior is fundamentally a problem of generalization - which is exactly what makes it so difficult to solve with current evaluation methods.

How do we move beyond monolithic generation to develop modular, composable systems that learn robust representations and flexibly combine distinct concepts?

I'm interested in generative models not primarily for their outputs, but for what they reveal about the structure of data - and for their role as a mathematical framework applicable far beyond image synthesis. Specific interests:

Generation

Energy-Based Models & Compositionality - Formulating generation as energy minimization to enable flexible combination of independent models and constraints at inference time (e.g., composing separate "object" and "lighting" models).

Diffusion & Flow Matching - Leveraging generative modeling to solve inverse problems, model continuous-time dynamics, and treat planning as conditional inference, while advancing the rigorous SDE and ODE mathematical foundations.

Representation

Self-Supervised Learning - Learning robust, disentangled representations from unlabeled data via masked modeling and contrastive objectives.

Structured Representations & Alignment - Investigating how models can learn combinatorial features that allow for robust generalization and grounding across modalities.

Why it's hard

There is no objective criterion for a "good" representation - one that is optimal for generation may be useless for reasoning, and disentanglement is extremely difficult to guarantee or even measure rigorously.

How do we build systems that genuinely reason - abstracting, deducing, and generalizing - rather than pattern-matching?

Reasoning is distinct from representation: a model can have excellent embeddings and still fail at multi-step deduction. I'm interested in what it takes to go from statistical regularity to structured inference. Specific interests:

Abstraction

Abstraction & Concept Formation - How models lift specific examples into generalizable concepts and apply them via analogical reasoning.

Chain-of-Thought & Self-Correction - Mechanisms for generating, critiquing, and refining internal reasoning traces before committing to an answer.

Robustness

Causal Reasoning - Moving beyond correlation to model cause-and-effect, enabling prediction of the downstream effects of interventions.

OOD Generalization - Ensuring reasoning stays robust when input distributions shift significantly from training.

Why it's hard

There is no agreed-upon benchmark that separates genuine reasoning from sophisticated pattern-matching - making it very difficult to know whether apparent progress reflects real improvement or benchmark exploitation.

How do we build internal simulations of the world rich enough to support planning and counterfactual reasoning?

Representation and reasoning are necessary but insufficient - an agent that can't anticipate consequences can't plan. World models provide that bridge, encoding not just what the world looks like, but how it changes. Specific interests:

Representation

Latent Dynamics Models - Learning efficient abstract representations of world evolution (e.g., Dreamer-style architectures).

Video-as-Simulation - Treating video generation as a path to learning intuitive physics and object permanence, not just visual fidelity.

Agency

Counterfactual Reasoning - Giving models the ability to imagine "what would have happened" under a different action or condition.

Model-Based RL - Using learned world models to plan policies entirely inside mental simulation before acting.

Why it's hard

A world model must compress the full complexity of physical reality into a latent space compact enough to simulate efficiently, yet rich enough to support accurate long-horizon prediction - and errors compound rapidly.

How do we ground intelligence in the physical world - creating agents that interact robustly with complex, unstructured environments?

The physical world is the ultimate stress-test: it is continuous, high-dimensional, and unforgiving. Policies that succeed in simulation fail on hardware. Robotics is compelling precisely because it makes hand-wavy claims about generalization impossible - either the robot picks up the object or it doesn't. Specific interests:

Control

Sim-to-Real Transfer - Training robust policies in high-speed physics simulations that generalize zero-shot to real hardware.

Visuomotor Control - Mapping raw visual observations directly to motor actions for fine-grained, end-to-end learned tasks.

Capability

Dexterous Manipulation - Enabling multi-fingered hands to handle objects with human-like precision and adaptability.

Active Perception - Agents that move to gather information, actively reducing uncertainty about their surroundings.

Why it's hard

The real world does not offer a reset button - distributional shift between simulation and hardware, sensor noise, and contact dynamics combine to make zero-shot generalization from learned policies extremely brittle.

In Progress, 2026

Mech Interp AI Safety

Unified Attribution: Bridging Mechanistic Interpretability and Data Influence for In-Context Learning

Shichang Zhang, Hugo Tierrablanca, Himabindu Lakkaraju

We design a unified framework that connects model behaviors to their origins by bridging mechanistic interpretability and data attribution. Our approach traces specific model outputs back to the joint effects of pre-training data, internal model components, and in-context information (RAG), aiming to disentangle parametric knowledge from retrieval-based context.

In Preparation, 2025

Evals AI Safety

Exploring the power of LLMs to solve the Selective Labels Problem

Hugo Tierrablanca, Martin Pawelczyk, Himabindu Lakkaraju

We tackle the selective labels problem in human decision-making by using Large Language Models to impute missing counterfactual outcomes masked by unobserved confounders. This approach allows us to estimate model risk on the full population, overcoming the bias inherent in evaluating only on the selectively labeled subset.
