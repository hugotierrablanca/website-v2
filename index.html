Hugo Tierrablanca

Research Associate @ Harvard University

Hi, I'm Hugo! I'm a Research Associate at Harvard University, working with Prof. Himabindu Lakkaraju on AI safety and reliable high-stakes decision systems.

My current work studies the selective labels problem and how to build faithful evaluation pipelines when labels are missing or biased. I am especially interested in combining interpretability, theory, and practical deployment constraints.

Previously, I graduated from Harvard University in May 2025 with a B.A. in Applied Mathematics. I am currently on a gap period and plan to apply to Ph.D. programs.

Research Interests

My research interests span AI safety and capabilities, with a focus on building AI systems that are both more powerful and more trustworthy. On the safety side, I am interested in how neural networks represent and process information, and how to control and align AI with human values. On the capabilities side, I am drawn to generative AI and world models as a path toward genuine reasoning, spatial understanding, and embodied intelligence.

Interests listed in no particular order.

I AI Safety

I'm interested in reverse-engineering the internal computations of neural networks - understanding how models store knowledge, form representations, and route information through circuits. Rather than treating models as black boxes, mechanistic interpretability asks how a specific behavior is computed, tracing it back to individual components and their interactions. My current work connects here through data attribution and influence functions: understanding why a model produces a given output by identifying which training examples most shaped that behavior.

Open Questions

Can influence functions reliably attribute model behavior to training data at scale?

Do circuits identified in small models transfer to larger architectures?

As AI systems grow more capable, ensuring they reliably pursue human-intended goals becomes increasingly critical - and increasingly difficult. I'm interested in the theoretical and empirical foundations of alignment: how do we specify what we want, how do we verify that a model has understood it, and how do we detect when it hasn't? My work on selective labels and evaluation bias is partly motivated by this: if we cannot faithfully evaluate a model's behavior, we cannot certify that it is aligned. Mechanistic interpretability and alignment are deeply connected - understanding internal representations is a prerequisite for scalable oversight.

Open Questions

How do we evaluate alignment when we can't observe all model behaviors in deployment?

Can mechanistic interpretability inform scalable oversight methods?

II Capabilities

My primary interest in generative models stems from their role as a core building block of world models - a model that can generate realistic future frames, scenes, or states is implicitly learning a structured prior over how the world evolves. I'm interested in what generative models actually learn about world structure, and whether that structure is rich enough to support downstream reasoning and planning. Separately, I find generative models compelling in AI for science: their ability to model complex high-dimensional distributions makes them powerful tools for molecular design and physical simulation, though this is not a current research focus.

Open Questions

Do generative models learn genuine world structure, or sophisticated interpolation?

How can generative priors improve sample efficiency in scientific discovery settings?

Current language models can produce impressively reasoned text, yet there is genuine debate about whether they reason or pattern-match. I believe internal world models - learned representations that support counterfactual simulation - are a plausible path toward genuine reasoning. A model that can mentally "run" a scenario before committing to an answer is doing something qualitatively different from next-token prediction. I'm interested in what architectural and training conditions give rise to such internal models, and how we might measure them rigorously.

Open Questions

What training conditions give rise to internal world models that support counterfactual simulation?

How do we measure whether a model is reasoning vs. sophisticated pattern-matching?

World models become most powerful when used for planning: an agent that can mentally simulate the consequences of its actions before taking them can reason about long-horizon goals in a way that reactive policies cannot. I'm interested in how learned world models can support more sample-efficient and generalizable robot policies, and what the demands of embodied intelligence reveal about the limits of current model architectures. Robotics is a demanding testbed precisely because the real world does not forgive distributional shift - making it an ideal stress-test for whether a world model is actually robust.

Open Questions

How can learned world models support long-horizon planning in continuous action spaces?

What does robotic failure reveal about the gaps in world model representations?

The physical world is fundamentally spatial - objects persist across viewpoints, move according to physical laws, and occupy structured 3D geometry. I'm interested in AI systems that form spatial world models: representations that encode the geometry, layout, and dynamics of scenes in ways that support robust perception and generalization. I follow work in this space closely, including research from World Labs and others pushing toward large-scale 3D and 4D world models trained from video - and I'm drawn to the question of what inductive biases allow models to acquire rich spatial understanding from inherently 2D observations.

Open Questions

What inductive biases allow models to acquire 3D geometric understanding from 2D video?

How does spatial world model quality correlate with downstream navigation performance?

In Progress, 2026

Mech Interp AI Safety

Unified Attribution: Bridging Mechanistic Interpretability and Data Influence for In-Context Learning

Shichang Zhang, Hugo Tierrablanca, Himabindu Lakkaraju

We design a unified framework that connects model behaviors to their origins by bridging mechanistic interpretability and data attribution. Our approach traces specific model outputs back to the joint effects of pre-training data, internal model components, and in-context information (RAG), aiming to disentangle parametric knowledge from retrieval-based context.

In Preparation, 2025

Evals AI Safety

Exploring the power of LLMs to solve the Selective Labels Problem

Hugo Tierrablanca, Martin Pawelczyk, Himabindu Lakkaraju

We tackle the selective labels problem in human decision-making by using Large Language Models to impute missing counterfactual outcomes masked by unobserved confounders. This approach allows us to estimate model risk on the full population, overcoming the bias inherent in evaluating only on the selectively labeled subset.
